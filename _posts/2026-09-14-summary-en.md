In a CoinDesk opinion piece published on September 13, 2026, MIT professor Muriel Médard argues that quantum-resistant blockchains should be built on mathematical innovations such as error-correcting codes rather than hardware-based quantum-proofing. She contends that algorithmic approaches offer more durable and scalable protection against future quantum attacks. As quantum computing advances, today's public-key cryptography — including the elliptic-curve schemes securing most blockchains — could eventually be broken, putting digital assets and transactions at risk. Médard's argument shifts the post-quantum debate toward software and mathematics, which could influence how blockchain developers and standards bodies prioritize their defenses. Error-correcting codes, such as Reed-Solomon codes, are mathematical constructions originally designed to detect and fix data transmission errors, and they also underpin certain cryptographic primitives like authentication codes that aim for unconditional security. Médard's position implies that these code-based techniques can be adapted to resist quantum cryptanalysis without relying on specialized quantum-hardware defenses.

**Background**: Post-quantum cryptography refers to cryptographic algorithms thought to be secure against attacks by quantum computers, which threaten widely used schemes like RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC). Blockchains rely heavily on ECC for signatures and key management, so the prospect of quantum decryption has spurred research into quantum-resistant alternatives. Error-correcting codes are a long-established branch of mathematics used in communications and, increasingly, in cryptographic design.

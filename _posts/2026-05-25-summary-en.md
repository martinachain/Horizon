## [Micro-roughness on airfoils reduces drag, overturning smooth surface dogma](https://www.wired.com/story/a-fundamental-principle-of-aeronautical-engineering-has-been-overturned/) ⭐️ 9.0/10

A new study reveals that applying micro-roughness to airfoils can reduce aerodynamic drag, contradicting the long-held belief that smoother surfaces always yield lower drag. This discovery could lead to significant fuel efficiency improvements in aircraft, automobiles, and high-speed trains by enabling a simple, low-cost drag reduction technique. The study found that micro-roughness in the transition zone of the boundary layer improves the drag coefficient, though the exact net improvement across the entire airfoil is not yet quantified.

**Background**: Aerodynamic drag is a major barrier to high-speed travel, and for decades engineers believed that smoother surfaces minimize drag. This principle guided the design of aircraft wings, car bodies, and train noses. The new research challenges this assumption by showing that controlled roughness can actually reduce drag, similar to how golf ball dimples reduce drag by promoting turbulent flow.

A new systematic study reveals that LLM agents suffer from 'constraint decay'—their performance drops significantly as architectural constraints accumulate, making them unreliable for production backend code generation. This finding challenges the assumption that LLM agents are ready for production-grade software development, highlighting a critical gap between unconstrained prototyping and real-world backend engineering. The study evaluated 10 language models across 8 web frameworks on 100 backend tasks, finding that capable agents lose an average of 30 percentage points in correctness as constraints increase.

**Background**: LLM agents are AI systems that autonomously generate code based on natural language prompts. While they excel at open-ended tasks like prototyping, production backend code requires strict adherence to architectural rules, database schemas, and other non-functional constraints that current benchmarks often overlook.

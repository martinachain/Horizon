## [Cactus Needle 3 ships 8-29MB automation models matching DeepSeek V4 Flash](https://cactuscompute.com/needle) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Cactus released Needle 3, a family of tool-calling and structured-JSON models that ship as 8-29MB 2-bit binaries and, on the Mobile Actions benchmark, the 20-layer variant scores 86.0 versus 82.4 for LFM2.5 1.2B and 76.0 for Qwen3.5 0.8B. The release adds intelligence laddering (every layer from 2 to 20 is a deployable subnetwork), a Monarch Hadamard MLP, multilingual support for seven languages, regex-based triggers, calibrated confidence scores, and broad platform support including WebAssembly and a WASI component. If tiny 8-29MB models can genuinely match much larger models on narrow automation tasks, on-device agents become practical on hardware like the Raspberry Pi 5, phones, watches, and browsers without cloud round-trips. This shifts the economics of tool-calling deployments toward local inference and task-specific fine-tuning rather than large general-purpose LLMs. Needle 3 deliberately does not chat; it returns an empty list when no declared tool fits, and it uses a rank-8 input-conditioned gate plus Walsh-Hadamard-initialized Kronecker (Monarch) factor pairs to cut FFN cost from O(d²) to O(d√d). Cactus claims DeepSeek V4 Flash-grade performance on a narrow task with just 4 layers of fine-tuning, and on a Raspberry Pi 5 the model decodes at up to 4k tokens/sec and prefills at up to 10k.

hackernews · HenryNdubuaku · Sep 18, 00:11 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49748553)

**Background**: Small language models trade general capability for size and speed, which makes them attractive for on-device automation but weak at open-ended chat. Tool calling means the model outputs a structured function call (e.g., turn on the lights) instead of prose, and structured JSON output means it emits machine-readable data. Needle 3 targets exactly these narrow formats, using architectural tricks like Monarch matrices and Walsh-Hadamard transforms to shrink the feed-forward network that normally dominates parameter count.

Ledger Donjon researchers demonstrated a photon-emission-guided laser fault injection attack that bypasses the RP2350 microcontroller's secure debug protection. By using differential photon-emission microscopy to localize the debug enable register and then applying a laser to flip two bits, they restored Secure debug access on an RP2350 A4 chip. This attack highlights the sophistication of modern hardware attacks and suggests that such techniques could be replicated with lower-cost equipment, potentially undermining the security of devices relying on the RP2350's secure enclave, such as YubiKey alternatives. It underscores the ongoing arms race between hardware attackers and defenders. The attack required approximately $250,000 in lab equipment for initial discovery and documentation, but community members estimate replication is feasible for under $25,000, possibly under $10,000. The technique involved differential photon-emission microscopy to narrow the laser search area before SWD-guided injection set the two bits required to restore Secure debug.

**Background**: Laser fault injection is a powerful active attack that uses a focused laser to induce faults in a chip's circuitry, often to bypass security mechanisms. Photon emission microscopy is a non-invasive technique that maps active transistors by detecting photons emitted when they switch, helping locate specific registers. The RP2350 is a microcontroller with a secure enclave designed to protect sensitive data and debug access, making it a target for security research.

OpenJev attempts to bring a similar decision-native interface to open models by reading option logits directly instead of generating an answer token.

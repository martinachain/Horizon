## [EDG open-sources its long-standing C++ front-end compiler](https://edgcpp.org/#transition) ⭐️ 9.0/10

EDG (Edison Design Group) has made the source code of its widely used C++ front-end compiler publicly available on GitHub under the Apache-2.0 license with LLVM exception, with The C++ Alliance serving as its nonprofit home. The open-source transition was announced on edgcpp.org, and the repository preserves commit history dating back to 1990. This is a major event for the C++ ecosystem because EDG's front end has been a foundational component in many commercial compilers and tools, including Microsoft Visual C++ IntelliSense, Intel C++ Compiler, and NVIDIA CUDA Compiler. Open-sourcing it could accelerate innovation in compiler tooling, static analysis, and language experimentation, and gives the community direct access to a historically influential codebase. The license is Apache-2.0 WITH LLVM-exception, which is permissive and compatible with LLVM's licensing model. The repository includes commit history from 1990 onward, an unusual feature for a newly open-sourced project, and documentation is available at edgcpp.org/doc. EDG's front end is not a standalone compiler but a parsing and semantic-analysis component that other vendors integrate with their own code generators.

hackernews · iandinwoodie · Sep 30, 19:26 · [Discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49913192)

**Background**: EDG, founded in 1988, is an American company that specializes in compiler front ends for C++ (and formerly Java and Fortran). A compiler front end handles preprocessing, parsing, and semantic analysis, producing an intermediate representation that a back end turns into machine code. EDG's front end is known for its strict standards conformance and has been licensed by over 180 commercial entities, making it one of the most influential pieces of C++ infrastructure.

## [Declassified: URSALA, RAQUEL, FARRAH Spy Satellites Revealed](https://www.thespacereview.com/article/4951/1) ⭐️ 7.0/10

The Space Review published a detailed history of the top-secret URSALA, RAQUEL, and FARRAH reconnaissance satellites, which operated from the 1970s into the 21st century as hitchhiker payloads on larger satellites. The article traces a secretive U.S. program that began in 1963 and lasted more than 40 years under various names and designations. The article highlights U.S. technological superiority during the Cold War and offers rare insight into signals-intelligence satellites that were long hidden from public view. It adds to a growing body of declassified material that helps historians and the public understand how space-based reconnaissance shaped national security. The satellites were about the size of a large suitcase, festooned with antennas, and spun rapidly in low Earth orbit to sweep their antennas over the ground for radar and electronic intelligence. URSALA IV, for example, was a 207 kg spacecraft built on the P-11 bus, launched in 1979 on the HEXAGON SV-15 mission, and operated by the NRO until a power failure in 1983.

**Background**: Reconnaissance satellites, informally called spy satellites, are Earth-observation or communications satellites deployed for intelligence gathering. During the Cold War, the U.S. developed both photographic satellites like the KH-9 HEXAGON and smaller signals-intelligence (ELINT) payloads that rode along as 'hitchhikers.' The National Reconnaissance Office (NRO) managed these programs, and many details remained classified for decades until documents were declassified and published.

## [Intracranial Recordings Reveal Spiral and Concentric Brain Waves During Memory Tasks](https://www.quantamagazine.org/surprisingly-complex-waves-reveal-the-brains-inner-workings-20260930/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Quanta Magazine reports on new intracranial recordings, published in Nature Communications, showing that the human brain produces surprisingly complex traveling waves — including spiral and concentric (expanding/contracting) patterns — during memory tasks. The study, from Jacobs' lab, used electrodes implanted in epilepsy patients to capture these wave shapes across multiple brain areas. The finding adds a new layer to the long-running debate over whether brain waves are merely epiphenomena of neuronal firing or actually drive information processing, and it could reshape how neuroscientists model memory formation. It also highlights the value of rare intracranial data from epilepsy patients for understanding cognition.
